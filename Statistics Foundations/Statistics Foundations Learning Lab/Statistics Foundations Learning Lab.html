Measures of Central Tendency

Learn about mean, median, and mode - the three main measures of central tendency.

📐 Mean (Average)

The mean is the arithmetic average of a dataset. It's calculated by summing all values and dividing by the count.

\[ \text{Mean} (\bar{x}) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i}{n} \]
📊 Marketing Example:
Customer Spending Analysis

Scenario: Analyze monthly spending of 8 customers:

Data: $120, $85, $95, $150, $110, $130, $75, $180

Calculation: Sum = $120 + $85 + $95 + $150 + $110 + $130 + $75 + $180 = $945
Mean = $945 ÷ 8 = $118.13

Interpretation: Average customer spends $118.13 per month

Measures of Dispersion

Learn how to measure the spread or variability in your data.

↔️ Range

The range is the simplest measure of dispersion: it's the difference between the maximum and minimum values.

\[ \text{Range} = \text{Max} - \text{Min} \]
📊 Marketing Example:
Daily Website Traffic Variability

Scenario: Compare traffic between two weeks:

Week 1: 850, 870, 890, 910, 930, 950, 970

Week 2: 500, 600, 900, 1200, 950, 700, 1100

Week 1 Range: 970 - 850 = 120 visitors

Week 2 Range: 1200 - 500 = 700 visitors

Interpretation: Week 2 has much more variable traffic!

Minimum
850
Maximum

Marketing Application: Range helps identify consistency. Low range means stable metrics, high range means variability that might need investigation.
📏 Variance & Standard Deviation

Variance measures average squared deviation from the mean. Standard deviation is its square root, in original units.

\[ \text{Variance} (\sigma^2) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{n} \] \[ \text{Standard Deviation} (\sigma) = \sqrt{\text{Variance}} \]
📊 Marketing Example:
Email Campaign Performance Consistency

Scenario: Compare click-through rates for two campaigns:

Campaign A: 2.1%, 2.3%, 2.0%, 2.2%, 2.1%, 2.3%

Campaign B: 1.5%, 2.8%, 0.9%, 3.2%, 1.8%, 2.6%

Both have mean: 2.17%

Campaign A SD: 0.12% (consistent)

Campaign B SD: 0.82% (variable)

Interpretation: Campaign A is more predictable!

Marketing Application: Low standard deviation means consistent performance. High standard deviation means unpredictable results that require investigation.
🔢 Z-Scores

A z-score tells you how many standard deviations a value is from the mean. Useful for comparing different datasets.

\[ z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \]
📊 Marketing Example:
Identifying Exceptional Sales Performance

Scenario: Monthly sales data with mean = $50,000 and SD = $8,000

Sales Rep A: $42,000 → z = -1.0 (below average)

Sales Rep B: $50,000 → z = 0.0 (average)

Sales Rep C: $66,000 → z = 2.0 (exceptional)

Interpretation: Rep C is 2 standard deviations above average - likely deserves recognition!

🔔 Normal Distribution

The normal distribution (bell curve) is symmetric around the mean with predictable proportions within standard deviations.

📊 Marketing Example:
Customer Purchase Amounts

Scenario: Online store purchase amounts follow normal distribution

Mean: $85 (average purchase)

Standard Deviation: $20

Interpretation:
• 68% of purchases: $65 - $105
• 95% of purchases: $45 - $125
• 99.7% of purchases: $25 - $145
• Purchases over $145 are rare (< 0.15%)

Marketing Use: Set realistic sales targets, identify outliers

Variable Types

Understanding different types of variables is essential for choosing appropriate analyses.

🔢 Quantitative Variables

Numerical variables that represent quantities or measurements.

📊 Marketing Examples:
Quantitative Marketing Metrics

Continuous Variables (can take decimal values):
• Conversion rate: 3.75%
• Average order value: $45.67
• Customer lifetime value: $1,245.89
• Bounce rate: 42.8%
• Session duration: 3.45 minutes

Discrete Variables (integer counts):
• Number of website visitors: 1,245
• Number of purchases: 89
• Number of clicks: 567
• Number of social media followers: 10,234
• Number of newsletter subscribers: 5,678

Continuous Variables

  • Can take any value within a range
  • Often measured or calculated
  • Examples: Height, weight, temperature, time
  • Can have decimal values
Statistical Analysis:
• Mean, median, mode
• Standard deviation
• Correlation, regression
• T-tests, ANOVA

Discrete Variables

  • Countable, integer values
  • Often counted
  • Examples: Number of children, cars, visits
  • No values between integers
Statistical Analysis:
• Frequency counts
• Proportions, percentages
• Chi-square tests
• Poisson regression

📋 Qualitative Variables

Categorical variables that represent qualities or characteristics.

📊 Marketing Examples:
Qualitative Marketing Data

Nominal Variables (no order):
• Gender: Male, Female, Other
• Marital status: Single, Married, Divorced
• Product category: Electronics, Clothing, Food
• Payment method: Credit card, PayPal, Cash

Ordinal Variables (ordered categories):

Nominal Variables

  • Categories with no inherent order
  • Used for classification
  • Can be coded as numbers but numbers have no mathematical meaning
  • Examples: Colors, cities, brands
Analysis Methods:
• Frequency tables
• Mode
• Chi-square tests
• Logistic regression

Ordinal Variables

  • Categories with meaningful order
  • Distance between categories not necessarily equal
  • Examples: Ratings, rankings, satisfaction levels
  • Can be treated as quantitative in some analyses
Analysis Methods:
• Median, mode
• Percentiles
• Non-parametric tests
• Ordinal regression

↔️ Independent vs Dependent Variables

Independent variables are predictors or causes, dependent variables are outcomes or effects.

📊 Marketing Examples:
Marketing Research Scenarios

Scenario 1: Pricing Experiment
• Independent: Price ($10, $15, $20)
• Dependent: Number of units sold
• Research Question: How does price affect sales?

Scenario 2: Ad Campaign Analysis
• Independent: Ad type (Video, Image, Text)
• Dependent: Click-through rate
• Research Question: Which ad type gets most clicks?

Scenario 3: Customer Segmentation
• Independent: Age, income, location
• Dependent: Purchase amount
• Research Question: Which customer characteristics predict spending?

Independent Variables

  • Also called: Predictors, Explanatory variables, Inputs
  • Manipulated or controlled by researcher
  • Come first in time sequence
  • Cause or influence the dependent variable
Marketing Examples:
• Advertising spend
• Price
• Product features
• Promotional offers

Dependent Variables

  • Also called: Response variables, Outcomes, Outputs
  • Measured or observed
  • Come after in time sequence
  • Affected by independent variables
Marketing Examples:
• Sales revenue
• Customer satisfaction
• Market share
• Brand awareness

Sampling Techniques

Learn different methods for selecting samples from populations.

🎯 Sampling Methods
📊 Marketing Examples:
Real-World Marketing Sampling

Simple Random Sampling:
• Selecting 200 random customers from 10,000 for a survey
• Using random number generator to choose participants

Stratified Sampling:
• Surveying customers proportionally by age group
• Ensuring sample represents all income levels

Cluster Sampling:
• Surveying all customers in randomly selected stores
• Testing ads in randomly selected cities

Systematic Sampling:
• Surveying every 10th website visitor
• Interviewing every 50th customer in database

Simple Random Sampling

Description: Every member has equal chance of selection

Marketing Example: Randomly select 50 customers from database of 5,000

Pros: Unbiased, simple to implement

Cons: May not represent subgroups, requires complete list

When to use: When population is homogeneous

Stratified Sampling

Description: Divide population into strata, sample from each

Marketing Example: Sample proportionally from different customer segments (age, income)

Pros: Ensures subgroup representation, more precise estimates

Cons: Need population information, more complex

When to use: When subgroups differ and need representation

Cluster Sampling

Description: Randomly select clusters, survey all in cluster

Marketing Example: Select random stores, survey all customers there

Pros: Cost-effective, practical for large populations

Cons: Less precise, clusters may not represent population

When to use: When population is geographically dispersed

Systematic Sampling

Description: Select every kth member

Marketing Example: Survey every 20th website visitor

Pros: Simple, evenly spread sample

Cons: May have hidden patterns, not truly random

When to use: When list is random and no hidden patterns

Determining how many participants you need for reliable results.

📊 Marketing Example:
Market Research Sample Size

Scenario: Company wants to estimate customer satisfaction

Confidence Level: 95% (standard)

Sample Size Formula: \[ n = \frac{Z^2 \times p \times (1-p)}{E^2} \] Where: Z = Z-score, p = proportion (0.5 for maximum), E = margin of error
⚠️ Bias in Sampling

Common biases that can distort survey results and how to avoid them.

📊 Marketing Examples:
Common Sampling Biases in Marketing

Selection Bias:
• Only surveying customers who made purchases (missing non-buyers)
• Online survey missing offline customers

Response Bias:
• Customers giving socially desirable answers
• Leading questions: "How much do you love our product?"

Non-response Bias:
• Only enthusiastic customers complete survey
• Busy professionals not responding

Survivorship Bias:
• Only analyzing successful campaigns
• Only studying current customers (ignoring churned)

Common Sampling Biases

Bias Type Marketing Example Solution
Selection Bias Only surveying website visitors Use multiple channels
Response Bias Leading questions Neutral wording
Non-response Bias Low survey completion Follow-ups, incentives
Survivorship Bias Only successful campaigns Include failures in analysis

How to Reduce Bias

  • Random sampling: Every member has equal chance
  • Pilot testing: Test survey on small group first
  • Multiple contacts: Follow up with non-respondents
  • Neutral questions: Avoid leading language
  • Multiple methods: Online, phone, in-person
  • Anonymous responses: Encourage honesty

❓ Null & Alternative Hypotheses

The foundation of hypothesis testing: stating what you're trying to prove and what you're comparing against.

📊 Marketing Examples:
Marketing Hypothesis Examples

1. Website Redesign Test:
• H₀: New design has same conversion rate as old (≤ 3.5%)
• H₁: New design has higher conversion rate than old (> 3.5%)
• One-tailed test (looking for improvement)

2. Pricing Experiment:
• H₀: New price has same sales as old price
• H₁: New price has different sales than old price
• Two-tailed test (could be better or worse)

3. Ad Campaign Test:
• H₀: Campaign A and B have equal click-through rates
• H₁: Campaign A has different click-through rate than B
• Testing for any difference

Null Hypothesis (H₀)

  • "No effect" or "no difference"
  • Default position
  • What we assume is true until proven otherwise
  • Example: "New design has same conversion rate as old"
Characteristics:
• Contains equality (=, ≤, ≥)
• Presumed true until evidence contradicts it
• What we try to reject with data

Alternative Hypothesis (H₁)

  • What you're trying to prove
  • Opposite of null hypothesis
  • Example: "New design has higher conversion rate"
  • Supported when we reject H₀
Characteristics:
• Contains inequality (≠, <, >)
• What we hope to demonstrate
• Can be one-tailed or two-tailed

❌ Type I & II Errors

Understanding the two types of errors in hypothesis testing and their business implications.

📊 Marketing Examples:
Real Business Consequences

Type I Error Example (False Positive):
• Declaring a new ad campaign successful when it's not
• Consequence: Wasting $50,000 on ineffective campaign
• Business impact: Lost budget, missed opportunities

Type II Error Example (False Negative):
• Failing to detect that a new feature increases retention
• Consequence: Missing out on 20% retention improvement
• Business impact: Lost revenue, competitive disadvantage

Type I Error (α)

False Positive

Rejecting null hypothesis when it's actually true

Marketing Example: Saying new design works when it doesn't

Probability: α level (usually 0.05)

Consequence: Wasting resources on ineffective changes

How to reduce: Use lower α (0.01 instead of 0.05)

Type II Error (β)

False Negative

Failing to reject null hypothesis when it's false

Marketing Example: Missing a truly effective campaign

Probability: β level

Power: 1-β (probability of detecting true effect)

Consequence: Missing profitable opportunities

How to reduce: Increase sample size, use higher power

📈 Linear Regression

Predicting a continuous outcome variable based on one or more predictor variables.

📊 Marketing Example:
Predicting Sales from Advertising Spend

Data: Monthly records for 12 months

Variables:
• Independent (X): Advertising spend ($000s)
• Dependent (Y): Sales revenue ($000s)

Interpretation:
• Intercept (15.2): $15,200 sales with $0 advertising
• Slope (2.8): Each $1,000 in ads generates $2,800 in sales
• R² = 0.85: 85% of sales variation explained by advertising
